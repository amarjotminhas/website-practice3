Straight answers before you start

Three things Portland homeowners ask us most, answered honestly.

  • What a kitchen or bath remodel really costs in Portland
  • How long permits actually take
  • Red flags when you're hiring a contractor

What a kitchen or bath remodel really costs in Portland

Most homeowners start with a number they saw online, and it's almost always too low for the work they actually want. That gap between the plan and the budget is where projects stall before they begin.

Here's the real range in Portland right now. A mid-grade kitchen remodel with new cabinets, quartz counters, and moved plumbing lands between $50,000 and $70,000. A full bathroom taken down to the studs runs $28,000 to $40,000. If you're only refacing cabinets and swapping counters with the layout left alone, you can come in under $30,000. The number climbs fast the moment you move a wall, relocate a gas line, or open a load-bearing span — a single flush structural beam can add $3,000 to $6,000 once you count the engineering and the labor to set it.

Budget another 10 to 15 percent on top of your quote for what the walls hide. In a house built before 1970, we plan on finding at least one of three things: old wiring, a corroded waste line, or subfloor rot. On a recent Irvington bathroom, hidden rot added $2,400 before we touched a single finish.

Before you fall in love with a tile, price the structural and mechanical work underneath it, because that's what moves the total — and ask for an itemized written quote so you can see exactly where the money goes.

How long permits actually take

The permit is the part of a remodel homeowners underestimate most. The build might take eight weeks, but the paperwork in front of it can add three or four before a single wall comes down.

In Portland, a straightforward trade permit — replacing a water heater or adding a simple electrical circuit — can often be issued the same day over the counter. A residential building permit for a room addition or a structural change is a different animal. Those go through plan review, and review times run two to six weeks depending on how busy the bureau is and whether your drawings need a structural engineer's stamp. A second-story addition almost always does. Add another week or two if the reviewer sends back a correction and the plans have to be revised and resubmitted.

Then there's inspection scheduling during the build. You typically need sign-offs at framing, at rough-in for electrical and plumbing, and at final. Each one has to be booked, and a rescheduled inspection can idle a crew for a day or two. On a Beaumont addition last year, one bumped framing inspection pushed the whole job about ten days.

If your project touches structure, plumbing, or electrical, ask your contractor for the current review time before you set a move-in date, and build that number into the front of your schedule instead of the end.

Red flags when you're hiring a contractor

A bad contractor rarely looks bad on day one. The warning signs show up in how they quote, what they ask for, and what they won't put in writing.

Start with the license. In Oregon, residential contractors must carry an active CCB number and be bonded and insured — ours is CCB #198442, and you can look it up. If someone can't give you a number that checks out, stop there. Next, watch the deposit. A reasonable deposit reserves your slot and covers initial materials; a request for 40 or 50 percent up front is a real problem, because it usually means your money is funding someone else's job, not yours. We bill in stages tied to progress — framing, rough-in, completion — never the full amount up front.

Be wary of a quote that's a single lump sum with no breakdown. An itemized quote shows you the cabinets versus the labor versus the permits, and it's the only honest way to compare bids. A number that comes in thousands below everyone else usually isn't a deal; it's a figure that will grow through change orders once the walls are open.

Finally, ask what happens when they find a problem behind the drywall. Anyone who says that never happens is either new or not being straight with you — the right answer is that they stop, show you, and put the cost in writing first.
